Check the delivery in

Keep the checks. Make comparison, acceptance and signing the learner’s work.

Recommended decision: approve an order-sheet-led design direction, not an app build. Retain three trolleys, ten quantity checks, six temperature checks and all four fish observations. Enter inspection results beside the evidence; give a few decisions depth and keep routine checks brief.

Intended outcome: the learner can explain what was ordered, what the supplier claims, what was checked, what is suitable to accept, and why the signed note must match that accepted amount.

Proposed workspace · 2 / 7

One working order sheet, three sources

Make the order sheet the persistent receiving workspace, grouped by the existing trolleys. Opening a row brings its box, scales/count evidence, probe and relevant guidance beside that row. The learner writes each measured result directly into the row once. Closing an inspection leaves the sheet, evidence links and unfinished work available.

Proposed journey

  1. Orient at the back door. Keep the approved situation, job and done-when wording. Make the order sheet and source documents immediately available; navigation does not name the fault.
  2. Try one normal line with support. Suggest smoked haddock on trolley 1 as the first practice line; show how to inspect, enter and compare without doing it for the learner.
  3. Work through the three trolleys. Finish each row beside its evidence. Allow revisiting and non-linear selection; keep all remaining checks visible.
  4. Explain the important decisions. Fish evidence, chilled-guidance comparison and packaging units get short focused prompts; routine lines need no written explanation.
  5. Prepare and send the discrepancy report. The learner calculates the shortage and reports it to Terence; report preparation can begin when the mismatch is found.
  6. Correct the supplier’s note. Choose the affected line and accepted amount, preserve the original claim and deliberately initial the correction.
  7. Review, sign, then sign off the task. Require all ten checks, six readings, four fish findings, decisions, report and correct amendment before the note can be signed. Preserve the existing separate task sign-off and read-only completed state.

Reasoning storyboards · 4 / 7

Three trolleys, three emphases

1 · Fish: distinguish “short” from “not suitable”

Salmon sequence: open the order and Exmouth note (both 12 kg) → inspect and weigh the delivered salmon (8 kg), take its temperature (2.1°C) → enter those observations → identify that both documents claim more than is present → calculate 12 − 8 = 4 kg missing → choose Short and deliberately accept the 8 kg of suitable goods.

The condition decision uses the scenario’s suitable-goods finding and its own temperature evidence, not the quantity mismatch. The learner must not refuse acceptable salmon merely because some is missing. Do not add a condition defect or make the driver identify the discrepancy before the learner attempts it.

Whole sea bass sequence: count 10, read 1.8°C, inspect all four supplied findings: clear/bright/slightly domed eyes; deep red/wet gills; clean sea-like smell; firm flesh that springs back. Then choose acceptance and answer once: “Which findings support your decision?” Select the relevant evidence and write one brief reason, not four repeated descriptions.

Expected explanation, for reviewers only: “The count matches, the temperature meets the supplied guidance, and the eyes, gills, smell and flesh findings support accepting the fish.” All four findings must be inspected. The reason should connect the evidence to the decision, rather than merely say “I checked it”.

Use eyes/gills on whole sea bass, not salmon fillets or smoked haddock. The current controls share four fish flags across fish boxes; do not claim they establish separate sensory checks for each product. Smell and texture are supplied scenario findings (“What you notice”), not sensations the browser detects. Image/text alternatives show the same evidence. Any additional fillet-specific evidence requires content approval.

2 · Chilled: interpret, rather than only copy, a reading

Check chicken, cream and butter separately, retaining all counts and readings. Use butter (4.4°C) for one short comparison with an always-available employer-supplied receiving guide: “Does this reading meet the receiving guidance?” The learner points to the applicable guidance and chooses acceptance; chicken and cream then use the same reference with reduced prompting.

Approval boundary: current data specifies 5°C or below for all six chilled lines, but this is not shown as learner-facing guidance. A proposed receiving card may use that existing scenario value only after the employer confirms its scope, wording and what to do outside it. Do not invent a threshold or import the fridge/chiller rules from other tasks. All six current readings meet the stored limit; butter is not a hidden refusal. Reading-entry tolerance and food acceptance criteria are different things. Until guidance is approved, do not describe this activity as teaching a validated workplace safety rule.

3 · Fruit and veg: compare the right packaging/count unit

Existing-facts option: inspect two 5 kg shallot sacks. Ask “Does the order ask for sacks or kilos?” Enter 2 sacks, not 10 in a sacks field. The same goods total 10 kg, but the order is for two sacks. Count spinach as 8 bags and parsley as 10 bunches; inspect and count all 60 lemons.

Optional new packaging proposal: show lemons in three trays of 20, retaining 60 lemons in the order, claim and delivery. Let the learner inspect the contents, not infer them solely from a carton label, then enter 60 each rather than 3 trays. This packaging is not current content. It needs approval and suitable evidence; the existing sack example is enough for the core recommendation.

No new refusal is needed. Most deliveries should be routine; the activity is not ten fault-finding tricks.
Learner-owned report and signature · 5 / 7

Attempt first; explain and correct second

Keep answer-bearing information out of navigation

Currently the fish guide gives the finding before the observation; later prompts explicitly give four kilos missing and the 12-to-8 amendment. The “Next” strip gives the same correction. The approved complication can appear after any nonblank salmon amount, because entering an amount also sets counted; correct comparison or a shortage attempt is not required. The driver reacts to 8, and the radio reply supplies the solution. [S2–S7, S9]

Proposed disclosure gate: keep approved strings unchanged, but withhold the answer-bearing complication, driver response and solution-bearing radio reply until the learner has inspected salmon, entered quantity/temperature, compared order and note, and attempted status, accepted amount, shortage and an amendment amount. “Attempted” need not mean correct. Preparation of the amendment happens on the sheet; the supplier note remains unsigned.

Then show the approved complication verbatim. It supplies the service context: tomorrow’s lunch needs salmon; tonight’s launch does not. Learners need not guess that fact. They finish their report using it. Terence’s existing answer-bearing reply follows the report attempt, as corrective confirmation rather than the first source of the learner’s decisions.

PROPOSED REPORT TO TERENCE — SHORT, STRUCTURED, LEARNER-AUTHORED

Facts: choose the affected product; reference the learner’s saved quantity and source claims. The missing amount is the learner’s calculation from the comparison, not auto-generated.

Your message: “What is missing, what can you accept, and what needs following up?”

Service affected: “Which meal needs this, and what do you need Terence to do?”

Acceptable example, for review/feedback only: “Terence, salmon is 4 kg short: the order and note say 12 kg but I checked 8 kg. The 8 kg delivered is suitable to accept. Tomorrow’s lunch needs the missing amount, not tonight’s launch. Can you follow up with the supplier? I’ll correct the note to the amount accepted before signing.”

Keep the report to a few sentences, not ten explanations. Sending the radio message is a deliberate action; clicking a radio alone is not evidence of reporting. Check its factual fields and meaning, allow plain-language variants, and do not require an exact phrase or silently treat any non-empty text as a correct explanation. Automated free-text judgement is not assumed by this recommendation.

Make the amendment a document decision, not another notebook pass

The learner selects salmon on the fish supplier’s note, strikes the original 12 kg, and chooses the accepted amount from their saved work, or enters an amendment themselves. The recommended single-entry route uses the learner’s own proposed amount only after an explicit “Use my amount” action. It never substitutes the hidden correct answer. Original claim, amended amount and initials stay visible. The learner signs for 8 kg accepted, not 12 claimed or 4 missing. Neither initials nor signature is applied without an explicit action.

Recover before sign-off

“Review before signing” identifies unresolved checks and contradictions with links to their sources. Retain wrong entries as editable drafts, allow reinspection and resending a corrected report, and recheck dependent decisions after a quantity changes; never silently repair them. If work changes after the note is signed but before task sign-off, require explicit re-signing. Once the task is signed off, preserve the existing frozen, read-only result and completion identifiers; review and hints must not mutate it.

Support and corrective feedback · 6 / 7

Teach the method, then reduce the prompts

One supported normal example: smoked haddock

  1. Show the order’s 3 kg and the fish note’s 3 kg as two labelled claims.
  2. Prompt “Weigh what came in.” The scale reveals 3 kg; the learner writes it in the row.
  3. Prompt “Take the temperature.” The learner waits, reads 2.4°C, writes it once and uses the approved receiving guidance when available.
  4. Ask “Does this match both pieces of paper?” and “Will you accept what you checked?” Leave quantity, status and acceptance unselected; the learner chooses them.
  5. After their attempt, explain why the matching quantity and suitable condition support All here / accept 3 kg. Keep this example available to reopen, never impose it as ten repeated scripts.

Next, salmon gets the comparison question but no arithmetic answer; sea bass gets the evidence question; butter gets one guidance comparison; shallots get one packaging question. Remaining rows keep evidence, fields and the reference available with only neutral navigation: “Open the next box”, “Check your entries”, “Prepare your report”.

How to judge the design in a later learner trial

  • Comparison: without the explicit answer hint, the learner distinguishes ordered/claimed 12 kg from observed 8 kg, calculates 4 kg missing and explains why 8 kg is accepted and signed for.
  • Evidence: explains fish acceptance using inspected findings; interprets a chilled reading using the supplied guidance; counts packaging in the order’s unit.
  • Communication: their short report names the discrepancy, suitable amount, tomorrow’s lunch and supplier follow-up. It does not invent an effect on tonight’s launch.
  • Coverage and recovery: no unchecked row disappears; all ten quantities, six temperatures and four fish observations are completed. A mistaken value/status/report/amendment can be repaired before sign-off; signed-off work stays read-only.
  • Accessibility and effort: the same evidence and decisions are reachable by phone and keyboard. Check whether duplicate entry and navigation decrease, not whether students finish faster under pressure. Observe assistance used; do not add a learner score or timer.

A correct final form alone is insufficient evidence of independent judgement. Ask “Why accept this?”, “What is missing?” and “Why amend to that amount?” after the attempt. Distinguish independent reasoning from an answer reached with explicit help; both can finish after correction. This is a proposed review method, not a claim that user testing has occurred.
